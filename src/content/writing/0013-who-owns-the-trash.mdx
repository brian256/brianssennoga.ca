My primary school headmaster, Mr. Daniel Nkaada — who also served as District Education Officer for Mpigi — wore glasses, often alongside a cream suit, and he epitomized what clean looked like. His inspections weren't just about whether you had actual black polished shoes at the assembly line. There was also the school matron, checking that you'd tucked your sheets right and left your slippers underneath, in order.

At Kibuli SS, Mwalimu Timothy was the Aga Khan House Master for part of my four years there. Every other Saturday, we had general cleaning in the morning. Dorm rooms scored marks, which aggregated into house cleaning marks, which eventually fed into the annual results for whichever house took the title of Champion. That was not the only incentive on offer. Mwalimu Tim once made me stay back for a job not well done — only to catch me arriving late to Saturday morning classes as a result, and cane me for that instead. You learned quickly which incentive you preferred, and you got your cleaning done first, and fast.

Forty-odd years after Nkaada's cream suit, a grown man is being caned on a street in Kisenyi for the same offence.

On [July 25, 2026](https://health.go.ug/government-launches-monthly-national-cleaning-days-to-boost-public-health/), Uganda held its first National Cleaning Day — a new law, three hours long, seven to ten in the morning, the last Saturday of every month from here on. Businesses shut. Vehicles off the road except emergencies. Police deployed to make sure of it. By Monday, the [Uganda Police Force was admitting "operational lapses"](https://www.ugandaonline.net/news/2026-07-27--kcca-deploys-undercover-team-to-combat-city-littering--84e7c0f54e20/) and promising an internal review, after a video went viral of an officer repeatedly caning a man who hadn't complied. Prime Minister Robinah Nabbanja framed the stakes plainly at the programme's launch: an estimated 75 percent of Uganda's disease burden is preventable, tracing back largely to poor sanitation, and — describing what happens to the waste the city fails to collect — she noted that "the remaining waste often blocks drainage channels, pollutes the environment and contributes to disease outbreaks" ([Office of the Prime Minister, July 2026](https://health.go.ug/government-launches-monthly-national-cleaning-days-to-boost-public-health/)).

Both things are true at once, which is the uncomfortable part. The instinct behind the law is not wrong. The compound sweeping rota wasn't wrong either. What's changed is who's standing over the broom.

## The scale that makes force look inevitable

Start with the number that makes the cane almost make sense, even if it doesn't excuse it. Kampala generates somewhere between 2,000 and 3,200 tonnes of solid waste a day, depending on whose count you use — the Prime Minister's office cites [2,000–2,500 tonnes with 40–60 percent collected](https://www.galaxyfm.co.ug/2026/07/24/national-cleaning-days-ugandans-to-spend-three-hours-monthly-clearing-garbage-drainages/); Kampala's own Lord Mayor, Ronald Balimwezo, puts daily generation closer to 3,200 tonnes against a city collection capacity of only about 770 — "the rest is either burnt or remains untouched," he said days before the launch ([Pulse Uganda](https://www.pulse.ug/story/we-are-not-ready-for-cleaning-day-lord-mayor-balimwezo-2026072505000524162)). Either count leaves a wide gap, and that gap is where drainage channels flood, mosquitoes breed, and wetlands quietly absorb what the trucks never reach. In August 2024, part of the Kiteezi landfill — the only licensed site serving Kampala — collapsed under its own unmanaged weight and buried the neighbourhood beneath it. More than [thirty people died](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/articles/cly8k506ygzo). Kampala's Mayor at the time, Erias Lukwago, called it plainly "a disaster [that] was bound to happen" — the city had been trying, and failing, to find a second site for years. That is the emergency this law is actually responding to. The footage that travels is a police officer's cane. The footage that should travel is a mountain of trash swallowing a neighbourhood because nobody built anywhere else to put it.

The gap between "cleaning" and "collecting" showed up again almost immediately. By the Sunday after the inaugural exercise, [piles of swept-up waste were still sitting uncollected](https://www.independent.co.ug/kcca-turns-to-online-feedback-as-garbage-heaps-linger-after-national-cleaning-day/) along major roads in Kasubi, Nankulabye, Kawempe, and half a dozen other neighbourhoods — because there weren't enough trucks, and the one major dumping facility serving the city was backed up with turnaround delays. Nakawa Division Mayor Nubian Li put the citizens' side of the bargain bluntly: "this is a massive public health hazard waiting to happen," he said, noting residents had done their part and government now needed to invest in the trucks and infrastructure to finish the job. A national cleaning day, in other words, produced a national collection problem — because sweeping something into a pile is not the same as taking it away, and Uganda hadn't yet built the second half of that sentence.

So the question isn't whether Uganda has a sanitation crisis. It clearly does. The question is why the state now has to stand where the headteacher once stood — and whether a police baton can do what a compound inspection used to do for free.

## Four countries, four philosophies

Uganda's cleaning day is being sold, gently, as "modeled on Rwanda" — as though Rwanda invented the idea and Uganda is simply the newest franchise. That framing does the policy no favours, because it invites the laziest possible dismissal: another East African government copying Kigali's authoritarian aesthetic. The truth is more interesting, and more useful. At least four countries have run this experiment, and they've landed in genuinely different places.

*[Rwanda's Umuganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umuganda)* has been mandatory since 2007 — not new, not improvised. Citizens between 18 and 65 are expected to show up on the last Saturday of the month; unexcused absence carries a fine of about 5,000 Rwandan francs, a few dollars that matter to most households. What rarely makes it into the "modeled on Rwanda" pitch is everything that surrounded the mandate: a nationwide ban on plastic bags that predates Umuganda's formalization by nearly a decade, thirty years of consistent enforcement, and a participation rate — by the government's own account — [north of 80 percent](https://borgenproject.org/community-service-in-rwanda/). Rwanda didn't just legislate a Saturday. It built an entire regulatory environment around it and gave it three decades to become instinct.

*[Nigeria](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2026/03/lagos-reintroduces-monthly-environmental-sanitation/)* got there first, and got there the hard way. Environmental Sanitation Day began in 1983 under General Buhari's military government. The regime fell within two years. The sanitation day didn't — it ran in Nigerian cities for three decades after the soldiers who started it were gone, folded so deeply into civic memory that Nigerians in their forties can still describe the movement ban and the ritual of clearing gutters before ten a.m. Then, in 2015, a Nigerian [court ruled the mandatory movement restriction unconstitutional](https://businessday.ng/life/article/mixed-reactions-greet-monthly-sanitation-reintroduction-in-lagos/), a straightforward violation of freedom of movement. Lagos formally cancelled its version in 2016. And then, in April [2026, Lagos brought it back](https://dailytrust.com/lagos-resumes-monthly-environmental-sanitation-after-10-years/) — this time without the movement restriction, banking on voluntary buy-in instead. Governor Babajide Sanwo-Olu was direct about the trade-off: "the enforcement mechanism may have changed, but the responsibility to maintain a clean and healthy environment has not changed." Thirty years to institutionalize it. Nine years to lose it in court. A decade to decide the waste problem still mattered even without the baton. That arc — mandate, backlash, retreat, redesigned revival — is the cautionary tale Uganda hasn't lived through yet, and the honest answer to anyone who thinks a court ruling or a viral video ends this kind of policy for good. It doesn't. It just forces the enforcement model to evolve.

*[Kenya](https://housingandurban.go.ke/national-hygiene-programme-kazi-mtaani/)* solved for the same problem with a different lever entirely. Kazi Mtaani pays young people — a few hundred shillings a day — to clear drains and collect garbage rather than compelling everyone to do it themselves. It's smaller in scope and inconsistent in funding, but it makes an important point by simply existing: a wage can produce the labour a cane is trying to extract by force. Nobody needs to be caned into a job they're being paid for.

*[Singapore](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=b2c2c063-431a-4aa3-8c8d-02dc3b2a117d)* is the case Uganda's critics and defenders both seem to have missed, and it's the most instructive of the four precisely because Singapore is not a cautionary tale about African governance — it's one of the wealthiest, most orderly countries on earth, and it ran almost exactly this playbook. On October 1, 1968, Lee Kuan Yew launched the Keep Singapore Clean campaign: fines used as a tool of social control for the first time, spot inspections, public competitions naming the dirtiest office, factory, and school, saturation media coverage. His reasoning was explicitly economic — he argued a clean city was a precondition for the investment and tourism a young nation needed. But here's the part that matters most for Uganda: Singapore never turned the campaign into a permanent recurring mandate. It ran the intense version once, for about a month, and then handed the job to permanent infrastructure — a dedicated Ministry of Environment, hawker centres that replaced informal street vending, real sewage systems, and littering fines embedded in ordinary law and [enforced year-round](https://www.myjoyonline.com/why-singapore-is-so-so-clean/) rather than one Saturday in four. The campaign was the bootstrap. The system was the point. Fifty-odd years on, Singapore doesn't need a mandatory cleaning day, because it built the thing the cleaning day was always meant to produce.

Line those four up and a pattern appears that "modeled on Rwanda" completely obscures: every country on this list eventually had to choose between making the *mandate* permanent or making the *infrastructure* permanent. Rwanda chose the mandate and has held it for thirty years through sustained political will. Nigeria chose the mandate, lost it in court, and rebuilt it without the coercion that got it struck down. Kenya sidestepped the mandate with a wage. Singapore ran the mandate briefly and then quietly retired it in favour of the system underneath. Uganda, as of July 25, 2026, hasn't chosen yet. It has a law, a police force under instruction to enforce it, and — by the Lord Mayor's own account — nowhere near enough trucks to finish what the law starts.

## Canada, from the household up

I live inside the system Uganda is trying to legislate into existence overnight, and it's worth saying plainly: nobody here is being enforced into it. It starts small and unpoliced. Green bin, blue bin, black bin — the first sort happens at the kitchen counter, long before anything reaches a dumpster. In Edmonton, that sort has a rhythm most residents never think about: the [black garbage cart is collected every two weeks](https://www.edmonton.ca/programs_services/garbage_waste/curbside-cart-collection), recycling in blue bags goes out weekly, and the green food-scraps cart alternates between weekly in the warmer months and every two weeks in winter — three separate truck routes, on three separate schedules, quietly running past every house in the city without anyone standing over a bin to make sure it happened. Flattening a cardboard box before it goes in the blue bag is a habit so minor it barely registers as civic virtue, and that's exactly the point — it isn't civic virtue anymore, it's just what you do, backed by a truck that will reliably show up. Former Mayor Don Iveson, defending the shift to this system, said he trusted Edmontonians to keep sorting because the city has "a high level of ambition around the environment and around waste management specifically" ([CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/edmonton-waste-management-1.5265224)). Walk into any Starbucks, or honestly most cafés in this country, and you'll find a menu of reusable cup discounts and bottle refill stations that has nothing to do with government compliance and everything to do with brand identity. Nobody is standing at the counter with a baton. The system doesn't need one — because the trucks are already coming, on schedule, whether or not anyone is watching.

## Bottle depots, Goodwill, ecostations — the next rings out

Widen the circle and the same pattern holds. Alberta posted an [85 percent return rate on beverage containers in 2024](https://shootinthebreeze.ca/beverage-container-returns/) — the highest in Canada — not through enforcement, but through a deposit that makes recycling pay for itself. Alberta's Minister of Environment and Protected Areas, Rebecca Schulz, put it simply: "Albertans are winners and these results prove it." Goodwill and similar donation networks turn "I don't need this anymore" into a community resource rather than a landfill entry. [Edmonton's Eco Stations](https://www.edmonton.ca/programs_services/garbage_waste/eco-stations) exist so the things that don't belong in a regular bin — batteries, electronics, hazardous waste, bulky furniture — have somewhere obvious and free to go. Three different incentive architectures, three different reasons to comply, and not a single police officer required for any of them. This is what Singapore's endpoint looks like once it's had forty years to compound: infrastructure so normalized it reads as personal virtue.

## The arable land irony

Here's the part that should embarrass both countries a little. A meaningful share of what Canadians pay their municipalities to engineer — green bin composting programs, food waste diversion, all of it — is something most Ugandan households already have the raw material for and mostly don't use: a backyard, and a country with some of the most fertile, underexploited arable land on the continent. Compost that would take real municipal infrastructure to manufacture in Edmonton just happens in a Kampala backyard, quietly, for free, if anyone bothered to direct it there instead of into a drainage channel.

## What's actually at stake underneath it all

None of this is abstract downstream. Peer-reviewed sampling in [Kampala's Nakivubo wetland](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10661-015-4689-x) — the channel that drains much of the city's wastewater into Lake Victoria's Murchison Bay — has found bacterial and heavy-metal contamination above national safety thresholds in the water, the sediment, and even in yams and sugarcane grown on the wetland's edges using that same water. That runoff doesn't stop at the wetland. It moves into a lake that [supports more than 40 million people](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0380133019301868) across three countries and carries a fishery — Nile perch chief among it — that Uganda depends on for both food and export revenue. Untreated sewage from Kampala, Kisumu, and Mwanza; degraded wetlands that used to filter it before it ever reached open water — the lake is absorbing decades of urban runoff that a three-hour Saturday, however well-enforced, cannot touch. That's the actual stakes of the sanitation question, and it's worth remembering while the national conversation is fixated on a police baton: the cane is a scandal. The wetland is the emergency. One gets a viral video. The other gets a slow, unwatched fish kill.

## Both compounds

In Nkumba, I swept the compound because I was young and impressionable and wanted my headmaster to look on me with favour. At Kibuli, I swept my dorm room for marks, but also for the sanity of knowing my bed wouldn't fail inspection. In Makindye, where I grew up, sanitation was aided by a backyard garden that took compost, a chicken coop that turned leftovers into feed, and whatever remained was burned. In Komamboga, I paid for a garbage service, and they often suggested that sorting garbage wasn't our job at home — someone at the landfill was doing that for a living, and it wasn't necessary for me. In Edmonton, seven years of habitual sorting means my family already had the foundations that let us assimilate.

Uganda might want to legislate this. But the sustainable, portable version is the one where cleaning is voluntary, ingrained at the earliest opportunity, secured at the most granular level first, then layered — from household to community to city — and backed by infrastructure that makes compliance easy, welcome, and operationally feasible.

Every country in this piece eventually made that choice, one way or another. Rwanda made the mandate durable enough that, thirty years on, it functions as infrastructure. Nigeria learned its mandate couldn't survive its own constitution and rebuilt around consent instead. Kenya decided a wage was cheaper, and kinder, than a cane. Singapore ran the mandate for a single month and spent the next five decades building everything the mandate had only gestured at. Uganda, three Saturdays in, is still holding the cane and mistaking it for the plan.

If it were listening, I'd tell it what Mwalimu Tim taught me by accident, decades before he could have known he was teaching it: force gets you compliance once, and only for as long as someone is watching. What gets you a clean country every month for thirty years is making the clean choice the easy one — the truck that comes on schedule whether or not anyone's grading you for it, the depot that pays you back for showing up, the backyard that already knows what to do with what's left over. Command the first Saturday, if a country feels it must. But spend the other three hundred and sixty-four days building the thing that makes the cane unnecessary on the fifth one.
