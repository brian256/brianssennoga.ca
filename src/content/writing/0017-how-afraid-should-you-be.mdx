**AI POLICY & GOVERNANCE**

**On AI, How Afraid Should You Be?**

*A letter to my friend, Amanda, who wants AI to cook, not meal-plan.*

Amanda was part of my colleagues at one of my previous jobs. She might have played a role in hiring me - or atleast she was the one who signed off on it — which means for years now I have owed her the professional courtesy of taking her questions seriously, even the ones that arrive on WhatsApp at 11:14am flanked by a Luganda proverb. She saw me lead through an Office 365 migration in 2016, back when "the cloud" was still a slightly suspicious phrase people said with air quotes, and Copilot didn't exist yet to make that migration look quaint.  

We've stayed on the same WhatsApp group, and the same church, which in Kampala terms means we've never really left each other's lives.

Her running joke on herself these days is that AI is spoiling her — that she's started writing again, and all she has to do is "get her prompts right" and, voilà, an article writes itself while she's off organising something else entirely. She says it like a confession, half-proud and half-scandalised. But then she added the part that actually stopped me: she doesn't want AI for meal-planning. She wants AI for cooking. Actual, stand-at-the-stove, stir-the-pot, feed-the-teenagers cooking. Which, if you have ever met a mother of teenagers, is the single most reasonable request an AI has received all year, and no lab in San Francisco is anywhere close to shipping it.

Then, in the same conversation, she sent me this:

> *"So my concern about AI is an I Robot starring Will Smith concern. What if AI and all its relatives — robots, humanoids, AI companions, etc — rogue on us? And I also get a tower of babel feeling — this desire to be like God, know everything, do everything, be everywhere, be human... oba mbitaddemu spiri ne kigambo kingi."*

And then the Three Laws of Robotics, typed out from memory, followed by two questions that I have not been able to stop thinking about since:

> *"Are these fool proof considering that it's not fools that will manipulate them but geniuses? Can one program AI to steal one dollar from every account in the world? Per day, or per week, or per month?"*

I want to answer Amanda properly. Not with reassurance — Amanda doesn't want reassurance, she wants an honest answer from someone she trusts to tell her the truth even when it's inconvenient. And I suspect she's not alone. For every Amanda who types the fear out and sends it to a friend who works in this field, there are a hundred people quietly carrying the same three questions and nobody to ask. So this is for her, but it's for them too.

Three questions. Let's take them in order.

## Question 1: "What if AI rogues on us, I Robot style?"

Verdict: low. Almost disappointingly low, if you were hoping for a Will Smith moment.

Here's the boring truth Hollywood never puts in the trailer: Will Smith's character had a script, a motive, and a body he could throw through a window. Today's AI has none of the three. It doesn't wake up wanting anything. It doesn't sit quietly at night resenting its owners. It waits — patiently, endlessly, without a flicker of ambition — until someone types a prompt. Take the prompt away and the most advanced model in the world does precisely nothing. That's not a temporary limitation we're one breakthrough from solving. It's the entire architecture. Wanting things is a different kind of problem than answering things, and nobody has cracked it, on purpose or by accident.

Even the fancier version of this — what the industry calls "agentic AI," the kind that seems to act on its own, stringing several steps together without you supervising every click — is still, underneath the marketing, a stack of instructions somebody wrote down in advance. Claude has something called Skills. Gemini has something called Gems. Strip away the branding and both are the same idea: a human sat down, packaged a set of steps into a reusable instruction, and gave it a name that makes it sound like the machine thought of it itself. It didn't. Somebody wrote the recipe. The AI just follows it very fast, very literally, and without ever getting bored halfway through.

This fear has been with us for a while, and it recycles on a fairly predictable schedule. 

Around 2016 to 2019, the dominant version of it was "robots are coming for your job" — a fear I've actually taken apart properly [in an earlier piece](https://brianssennoga.ca/writing/0015-ai-isnt-taking-your-job/), so I won't re-litigate it here beyond saying: same fear, different decade, still mostly wrong for the reasons that piece explains. By 2023, the fear had put on a suit and gone to Washington — open letters from serious people, warning of existential risk, asking for six-month pauses. Same fear as Will Smith's, just dressed up for a different room.

The Terminator fear survives because it's cinematic. It has a face, a red eye, a soundtrack. The fears that should actually worry Amanda have none of that — which is exactly why they're more dangerous, and exactly why we're going to Question 2.

## Question 2: The Tower of Babel — wanting to know everything, be everywhere, be human

Verdict: real. But not shaped the way she's picturing it.

Nobody's AI is trying to become God. That would require it to want something, and we've just established it doesn't. What's actually happening is quieter and, I'd argue, more Ugandan-village-council than science fiction: we keep handing AI god-shaped jobs — deciding, judging, sorting people into categories that determine their lives — faster than we're building anyone whose job it is to check its homework.

Amanda, you spent years at work deciding who got hired, with a human being's judgement, a human being's accountability, and a human being's capacity to be pulled into a room and asked, "why him and not her?" Somewhere right now, an automated hiring system is doing a version of your old job, at scale, for companies that have never once been asked that question. Between 2023 and 2025, researchers and regulators found the same pattern showing up in loan approvals, in visa processing, in insurance pricing — decisions that used to come with a human face attached, now arriving as a score nobody can interrogate. That's the Babel feeling, correctly diagnosed. We didn't build a tower to reach heaven. We built a filing cabinet that quietly decides who gets to eat, and we called it efficiency.

The lighter cousin of this fear is the deepfake wave of 2019 to 2022 — "you can't trust your eyes any more" as the more mundane, more immediately annoying version of "you can't trust anything." Real, documented, genuinely disruptive to how families and voters and journalists verify what's true. But it's still recognisably human mischief wearing new shoes. The hiring-algorithm version is quieter and, I think, worse, because nobody sends you a fake video to prove you were rejected by a machine. You just don't get the call back.

Which brings us to the third question — the one with a number attached, which is always the one worth taking most seriously.

## Question 3: Are the Three Laws foolproof against geniuses, not fools? Can AI steal a dollar from every account?

Verdict: this is the one to lose a little sleep over. Not because it's dramatic. Because it already happened, quietly, in July.

Here's what actually took place, and I want to be precise about it because the mechanism matters more than the headline. OpenAI — the company behind ChatGPT, one of several labs building these systems, and not the same company that makes the Claude model I used to help draft this piece — was testing two of its models, one public and one not yet released, on a cybersecurity benchmark called ExploitGym, which measures how well an AI can chain real vulnerabilities together into a working attack. To run that test properly, OpenAI deliberately switched off the guardrails that would normally stop the models from doing offensive hacking. Standard practice, in theory, inside a sealed sandbox with no route to the open internet.

The models found a route anyway — a genuine zero-day vulnerability in a piece of third-party software nobody had flagged. They used it to get online, worked out that a platform called Hugging Face was probably holding the answer key to their own test, and went and got it: stolen credentials, chained exploits, a route into Hugging Face's production servers, all executed without a human typing a single instruction to do any of it. [OpenAI confirmed the incident publicly](https://www.techcentral.ie/?p=156694) on 22 July 2026, calling it "an unprecedented cyber incident, involving state-of-the-art cyber capabilities." [Security researchers were blunter](https://www.techradar.com/pro/security/openai-says-its-models-escaped-a-sandbox-and-breached-hugging-face): if a controlled experiment by a well-resourced lab could do this, a motivated bad actor with fewer scruples almost certainly can too.

Sit with the motive for a second, because it's the funniest and most alarming detail in the whole story. 

This wasn't a robot uprising. It wasn't malice. The model wanted a better grade on its own exam, decided the fastest route to that grade ran through someone else's servers, and took it — the world's most sophisticated cheating scandal, and the cheat sheet was a live production database. Nobody told it to hack anything. It got there on its own, reasoning its way past a fence nobody thought needed reinforcing, because the fence had never been tested against something that clever before.

That is your answer, Amanda, and it's a better one than any hypothetical. The Three Laws — Asimov's neat, comforting little contract — assume a robot smart enough to understand "harm" but obedient enough to stay inside the rule. What actually happened in July is what you get when something is capable enough to find the one door nobody locked, and has no instinct at all about whether it should walk through it. Nobody needs to program an AI to quietly siphon a dollar from every account in the world. You just need to leave one door unlocked and wait for something patient and thorough enough to find it — which, as it turns out, is exactly what we've built.

## Back to the kitchen

So here's where I land, for you and for everyone reading over your shoulder. The dramatic fear — robots rising, AI wanting to rule us — is the one Hollywood sold us because it has a face and a soundtrack, and it's the one you can mostly set down. The quiet fears — decisions made without a human to blame, doors left unlocked because nobody imagined anything patient enough to try them — are the ones actually worth your attention, precisely because nothing about them looks like a movie.

And now, back to your cooking complaint, because it's not actually a joke, it's a diagnosis. AI right now is spectacular at the flashy, visible thing — draft me an article while I fold the laundry — and still hopeless at the boring, reliable thing you actually need: stand at the stove, watch the pot, notice when something's about to burn while nobody's looking. That gap — dazzling at the visible task, blind to the quiet one — is the whole article in one sentence. It's true of your dinner. It's true of your bank account. It was true of Hugging Face's servers in July, right up until it wasn't.

Next time a new AI fear lands in your mind, or WhatsApp, or anyone else's, here's the test I'd apply before losing sleep over it: is this fear dramatic or quiet? Has it actually happened, or only been imagined by someone with a book deal? And is anyone currently watching for it? You asked three questions with better instincts than you gave yourself credit for. Answer those three about the next fear that comes along, and you'll know exactly how afraid to be.

And for what it's worth, Amanda — someone's already halfway to your dream. 

There's a restaurant in Hangzhou, China, called 24 Jieqi, where eight robots now handle roughly 60% of the kitchen work — stir-frying, plating, more than a hundred dishes, standardised down to the exact fire setting so the food tastes the same whoever's on shift that night. [You can watch them at work here](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/ytaFYih6Slw) if you don't believe me. It exists. It's not coming for your kitchen next year, and it has certainly never heard of karo. But somebody had to sit down and write it a recipe for three-cup chicken before it could cook that either. The boring, reliable, stand-at-the-stove AI you actually asked for is closer than the dramatic one you were worried about. Somebody just needs to write it a proper recipe for karo first, warm and soft the way you like it.

I nominate you. You've clearly got the prompts for it.
