**AI POLICY & GOVERNANCE**

**Africa Is Building the Roof Before the Walls**

*The African Union has drawn a continental blueprint for AI. Its member states are still laying bricks. The gap between the two is where the real work is.*

## Two ways to build a house

In Uganda, a house is not built. It is grown.

I've watched it happen in my own family. One year the foundation goes in, a neat grey rectangle on red earth, and then nothing, sometimes for a long time. The next year, when school fees are paid and a little is left over, the walls rise. A harvest, a bonus or a relative abroad sends money, and the iron sheets go on. Windows come later. Plaster comes much later. Somewhere along the way, someone moves in, because a house with a roof and no paint is still a house.

Drive through almost any Ugandan town and you'll see dozens of these: foundations waiting, walls without roofs, roofs without windows. To an outsider they can look like abandoned projects. To a Ugandan they look like patience. Each one is going up at exactly the speed its owner can afford, to fit exactly the family that will live in it.

Now I live in Edmonton, and at the edge of my own neighbourhood I watch the opposite happen. A large developer arrives with a master plan. Roads are paved before anyone lives there. Show homes go up, furnished and lit, with a catalogue of floor plans. Within a couple of years, a whole neighbourhood appears. It is fast, consistent and polished. You choose from the plans on offer; the plan does not ask about you. And sometimes the lots sell faster than the schools, clinics and bus routes can follow, and the neighbourhood is oversold before it is truly lived in.

This month, the African Union chose the Edmonton model for AI governance. Its member states are building the Ugandan way.

That is not a failure. It may be the most honest signal African AI governance has sent in years.

## The show home

In October, the African Union [unveiled its first continental AI governance framework](https://af.net/realtime/african-union-unveils-first-continental-ai-governance-framework/), aiming to harmonise AI policy across all 55 member states. It builds on the [Continental AI Strategy endorsed in July 2024](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-african-union), whose first phase, running through 2025 and 2026, is about setting up governance structures and national strategies, not enforcing rules.

On paper, this is the show home: well lit, carefully furnished, designed to make you picture life inside.

Now walk down the street. Africa has [almost no binding AI-specific law](https://www.levellers.ai/what-is/ai-regulation-in-africa). By mid-2025, only 16 of the continent's 54 countries had even published a national AI strategy. Most plots on this estate are still bare earth.

You can harmonise policies that exist. You can't harmonise a silence.

To be fair, the master plan matters. Roads have to be agreed before houses can connect to them. A shared continental vocabulary means a startup in Accra and a regulator in Nairobi are at least speaking the same language about risk. And there is a sovereignty argument too. Without an African plan, the default is to import someone else's floor plan whole, usually Brussels' or Washington's, and live in a house designed for a different climate.

But a show home is a promise, not a place to live. The real question is what's happening on the plots.

## Five plots, five builders

**Kenya is pouring the foundation before ordering the roof.** More on Kenya below, because it is the most instructive plot on the street.

**Nigeria is buying iron sheets before the walls are up.** It has a [National AI Strategy and no AI-specific law yet](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/report/nigeria-ai-governance-approach), but it is [widely expected to become one of the first African countries to legislate](https://www.levellers.ai/what-is/ai-regulation-in-africa). That speed could pay off. It could also leave Nigeria with a gleaming roof balanced on scaffolding: rules on the books, without regulators trained, funded and staffed to enforce them.

**Rwanda finished its walls, plastered them and moved in.** Its National AI Policy, approved by Cabinet in 2023, is [widely described as the first comprehensive national AI policy in Africa](https://www.levellers.ai/what-is/ai-regulation-in-africa). It isn't law. It doesn't need to be yet. It is being used, and that has given a small country an outsized voice: Kigali [hosts the secretariat of the Africa AI Council](https://techmoonshot.com/2026/05/26/africa-ai-regulation-2026-a-country-by-country-map-of-whos-ahead-and-whos-stalling/). Rwanda shows that a lived-in house persuades more than a blueprint does.

**Egypt has scaffolding up and a building permit pending, on purpose.** In March, its National Council for AI [published a National AI Governance Framework](https://www.middleeastainews.com/p/egypt-publishes-ai-governance-framework) alongside its National AI Strategy for 2025–2030, and said plainly that the framework is meant to inform the drafting of a future Egyptian AI law. Framework now, law when ready. That isn't hesitation. It's a construction schedule.

**Morocco ordered a Canadian floor plan for a plot it hasn't fully surveyed.** A draft AI law before its House of Representatives [takes its cues from the EU AI Act and carries criminal penalties of up to five years in prison](https://en.hespress.com/100403-100403.html), while Morocco's own [national AI strategy is still in preparation](https://aipolicytracker.org/ai-regulation-africa). Borrowed designs aren't wrong; sometimes the neighbours have solved a problem you share. But a house drawn for Europe's weather may leak in Moroccan rain.

Then there is the cautionary tale next door. In April, South Africa [withdrew its draft national AI policy](https://thenextweb.com/news/south-africa-ai-policy-hallucinated-citations) after journalists found that at least six of its 67 academic citations didn't exist. They were AI-generated inventions, citing real journals. The country was writing rules for AI, and AI wrote its footnotes. The policy has since been [pushed into 2027](https://technext24.com/2026/05/26/south-africa-pushes-ai-policy-to-2027/) while an independent expert panel rebuilds it. In house terms, it is discovering mid-build that the land title was never properly registered. The walls might be fine, but nobody will move in until the paperwork is real.

Five plots, five speeds, one continental master plan.

## Kenya didn't flinch. It sequenced.

In the same week the AU unveiled its framework, Kenya's Senate ICT committee recommended against the country's AI Bill, 2026. After [hearing from 143 stakeholders](https://www.africaainews.com/p/afdbs-10b-africa-ai-plan-kenyan-senate), the committee concluded the Bill might regulate AI ["too broadly, too early and too punitively"](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2026-10-15-senate-committee-shoots-down-nyamus-bill-on-ai) for where Kenya's ecosystem stands. Its advice was to finish the national AI policy first, which the Ministry of ICT says is in its final stages, and then legislate.

The lazy reading is that a government blinked under industry pressure. I don't buy it.

Picture a two-person agritech startup in Nakuru. They have built an app that tells a farmer, from a single phone photo, whether her maize has a disease and what to do about it. They run on open-source tools, a small grant and a great deal of optimism. Under the proposed Bill, a violation could cost [KSh 5 million or two years in prison](https://www.lawyershub.org/news/post/ai-policy-dialogue-recap-navigating-kenya-s-proposed-artificial-intelligence-bill-2026). For them, that fine isn't a deterrent. It's a death certificate. For a global tech giant, the same fine is an afternoon's rounding error.

Rules that only small builders feel are not building codes. They are a fence that hands the estate to the biggest developers.

What the committee actually said was: policy first, then law. Anyone who works in AI governance will recognise that order, because it is how our own toolkits work. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 don't open with penalties. They open with a management system: know what AI you run, assign who owns it, map the risks, set controls, keep records. Binding obligations make sense only once everyone understands what "compliant" looks like in practice.

Every Ugandan builder knows this without reading a standard. You don't hire the roofer until the walls can carry the weight. Building inspectors are essential, but they inspect what has been built. They can't inspect a brochure.

A law that arrives before its framework doesn't produce governance. It produces paperwork and penalties, and the paperwork falls hardest on whoever can least afford a lawyer.

South Africa's citation scandal makes the same point from the other side. If a policy shop can't verify 67 references, it isn't ready to audit high-risk AI systems in hospitals and banks. Capacity is the foundation, and you can't legislate a foundation into existence.

## What both builders get right, and wrong

I don't want to romanticise the half-finished house, or sneer at the show home. Both have something to teach.

The **master-planned estate** gets the roads right. Shared infrastructure, consistent standards and speed: a buyer knows what they're getting, and services can be planned at scale. Its weakness is that one plan serves every family, and the plan can outrun the people. An oversold neighbourhood looks finished from the highway and feels unfinished at the school gate.

The **house grown in stages** gets the fit right. Every room exists because the family needed it and could pay for it. Nothing is built for show. Its weakness is that it is slow and uneven, and sometimes it stalls for years when the money runs out. And without a common road, each house is an island.

Good AI governance needs both: a master plan for the roads, and owner-built houses on the plots.

That suggests a different role for the African Union. Less property developer selling finished homes, more planning authority. Survey the plots. Agree the roads and building codes: shared definitions, risk categories, cross-border data rules. Then let each country build at the speed its institutions can bear. Judge success not by how many laws are passed, but by how many houses people actually live in.

## So what, if you work in AI governance

If you build, buy or deploy AI in African markets, don't wait for the continental framework to become binding before you act. Build your own foundations now. Map your AI systems against ISO/IEC 42001 or the NIST AI RMF. Know what you run, who owns it and what could go wrong. When Nairobi, Lagos or Kigali do legislate, and they will, the organisations with their walls already up will be the ones moving in, while everyone else is still waiting for the roofer.

If you're watching from elsewhere, take the lesson seriously. Canada's own federal AI bill died on the order paper in 2025; quietly, we have been building the Ugandan way too.

The African Union has built a beautiful show home. Across the continent, countries are laying bricks one season at a time.

Show homes sell the vision. Foundations hold the weight.
